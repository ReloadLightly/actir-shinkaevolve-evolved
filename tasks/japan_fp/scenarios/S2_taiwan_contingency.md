Somewhere between 2027 and 2029, cross-strait deterrence fails. The trigger is
a political one — a Taiwanese electoral outcome Beijing reads as decisive, and
a window it judges to be closing. The contingency begins as a quarantine:
customs inspection of shipping bound for Taiwan, enforced by coast guard
vessels with naval forces behind them, accompanied by large-scale exercises,
cable cuts, and sustained cyber operations against Taiwanese infrastructure.
It escalates unevenly over months, with periods of standoff punctuated by
incidents at sea and in the air. Whether it culminates in blockade or invasion
is not settled within the window.

The regional consequences arrive immediately and regardless of the military
outcome. Shipping insurance rates spike across the first island chain.
Semiconductor supply is disrupted at global scale. Energy shipments through
the South China Sea are rerouted. Financial markets price a war premium into
the whole region.

The United States mobilises. Its posture questions are acute and public:
which bases, whose consent, how fast. Japan's Nansei islands, its host-nation
support arrangements, and its legal thresholds for the exercise of collective
self-defence all become operationally decisive rather than theoretical. Japan
faces simultaneous demands on force readiness, civilian protection, energy and
food resilience, alliance coordination, and diplomatic positioning with
partners who do not all take the same side.

Choices made before the contingency determine what is available during it.
