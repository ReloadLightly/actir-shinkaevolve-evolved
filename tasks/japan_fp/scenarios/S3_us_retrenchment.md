# S3 — US retrenchment and the transactional turn

Between 2026 and 2030 the United States turns inward and transactional. It
does not formally abandon its alliances, and the treaty texts are untouched —
but the assumptions underneath them thin out. Host-nation support demands rise
sharply and are linked explicitly to trade concessions. Force posture reviews
trim forward-deployed units. Extended deterrence is reaffirmed in language
that allies read as increasingly conditional, and consultation gives way to
notification.

Tariffs are applied to allies as readily as to rivals. Multilateral economic
arrangements are treated as costs rather than assets, and US participation in
regional institutions becomes intermittent. Technology controls are still
demanded of partners, but the reciprocal market access that made them
palatable is withdrawn.

Regional states respond by hedging faster. South Korea's domestic debate over
independent nuclear capability moves from the fringe toward the mainstream.
Australia's AUKUS timelines slip and the political consensus behind them
frays. ASEAN accommodates Beijing more openly. China does not need to escalate
militarily; the political ground shifts toward it without a shot fired.

For Japan the question is not whether the alliance survives on paper — it
does — but what capability, deterrent credibility, and regional standing it
retains when American commitment becomes negotiable. Every dependency
becomes a liability, and every partnership outside Washington becomes
comparatively more valuable. Autonomy, coalition-building, and resilience are
tested simultaneously.
