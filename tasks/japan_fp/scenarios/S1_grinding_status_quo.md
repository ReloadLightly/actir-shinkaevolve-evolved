Between 2026 and 2030 the regional order bends without breaking. US–China
competition intensifies across technology, export controls, and maritime
presence, but neither side seeks rupture and neither concedes. Tariff rounds,
entity-list expansions, and rare-earth and semiconductor bottlenecks recur on
a roughly annual rhythm; each is absorbed, none is decisive.

China's economy grows more slowly than in the 2010s but remains the region's
gravitational centre for trade. Its naval and coast guard presence in the East
and South China Seas continues to expand incrementally. Grey-zone pressure
around the Senkakus and in the Taiwan Strait becomes routine — more hulls,
more days on station, more airspace incursions — without crossing into
sustained armed conflict. Taiwan holds. Cross-strait tension is chronic rather
than acute.

The United States remains committed to its alliances and to forward presence
in Asia, though it presses allies harder on burden sharing and on alignment
with its technology controls. Extended deterrence commitments hold. The
Korean peninsula is tense but stable; North Korea continues testing.

ASEAN states hedge more openly, declining to choose sides and courting
investment from every direction. India rises steadily as an economic and
military weight without joining any bloc. Multilateral institutions continue
to function, slowly and imperfectly.

For Japan this is a world of accumulating pressure without a forcing event:
no crisis compels transformation, and no respite permits complacency. Effort
compounds or decays over five years, and the returns to any given policy are
gradual rather than dramatic.
